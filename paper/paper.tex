\begin{abstract}
AI coding agents have enabled a new class of developers who commit code at volumes an order of magnitude above historical norms, yet the commit-level behavior of these hyperactive AI-augmented developers remains unstudied.
We present the first longitudinal, commit-level study of such developers on GitHub, based on a curated dataset of seven individuals observed over six months (September 2025 to February 2026): 76,749 commits across 260 repositories, with per-commit file-level detail.
We address four research questions covering temporal activity patterns, coding agent usage and co-evolution, repository-switching behavior, and commit quality.
Our key findings are: (1) hyperactive developers follow divergent trajectories, ranging from sustained monotonic growth to sharp burst-collapse patterns, revealing that high volume is not synonymous with sustained engagement; (2) agent attribution is detectable in 47.6\% of commits, with \texttt{claude\_code} accounting for the majority (36,493 commits), yet two of our seven developers show near-zero hard agent signals despite extreme commit volumes, exposing a critical gap in current detection heuristics; (3) AI agents enable fluid, within-day repository switching at a scale unseen in pre-agent studies; and (4) commit quality signals diverge substantially between agent-attributed and non-attributed commits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}

% Agent adoption at scale
\begin{itemize}
  \item Robbes et al.~\cite{Robbes2026}: first large-scale study of coding-agent adoption on GitHub (129k projects); 15.85\%--22.60\% adoption; agent-assisted commits are larger and more feature/bugfix-oriented; introduces the \texttt{agent-mining} heuristics library used in our study.
  \item Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2026}: measurable LLM-induced shifts in coding style (snake\_case, complexity, maintainability) across 20k+ repositories linked to arXiv papers.

% LLM-generated code and self-admission
\begin{itemize}

% AI agent PRs
\begin{itemize}
  \item Alam et al.~\cite{Alam2026}: use the AIDev dataset; analysis of 8,106 fix-related PRs from five AI coding agents; investigates why agent-involved PRs remain unmerged.
  \item Ogenrwot and Businge~\cite{ogenrwot2026ai}: use the AIDev dataset to study how human and agentic PRs differ.
\end{itemize}

% Gap addressed by this work
\begin{itemize}
  \item No prior work studies the \emph{individual} developer trajectory at commit resolution over months; our case-study approach is complementary to population-level studies.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

\subsection*{RQ3 — Repository Switching Behavior (@Martin and team)}
\emph{How do hyperactive AI-augmented developers switch across repositories?}

Prior work suggests that developers seek to multitask across projects, but that excessive multitasking may negatively affect productivity~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky}. While most developers contribute to multiple repositories at least occasionally, sustained day-to-day multitasking across repositories remains comparatively uncommon.
%
AI coding agents may alter these dynamics by autonomously executing implementation tasks after instruction, allowing developers to work on other repositories while the agent continues operating. Combined with higher-level prompting interfaces that require less continuous repository-specific interaction, this may reduce the practical costs of switching between repositories and enable more fluid multi-repository workflows.
%
We hypothesize that reduced context-switching costs in AI-agent-assisted workflows are associated with higher within-day repository diversity, and lower concentration relative to pre-agent baselines (cf. Section~\ref{sec:baseline}).

A repository switch is defined as two consecutive commits targeting different repositories.
%
We analyze repository-switching behavior using three complementary measures:
(i) the number of actively maintained repositories per developer-week, defined as repositories receiving at least two commits within the week based on prior work~\cite{Damota2022deep}; higher values may indicate broader concurrent activity enabled by lower context-switching costs; %make it a week
(ii) repository concentration measured using the Gini coefficient, where values near 1 indicate activity concentrated in a small number of repositories and lower values indicate more evenly distributed work; and %measured over a week
(iii) repository switch counts at the day and session levels, where unusually high values may reflect highly fragmented or strongly parallelized development workflows. We segment activity into sessions using a 30-minute inactivity threshold, per prior work on developer activity sessionization~\cite{Mozannar2024when}.
%
Together, these measures allow us to assess whether AI-agent-assisted workflows are associated with broader and more fluid multi-repository development behavior relative to pre-agent baselines.

\subsection*{RQ4 — Commit Quality (@Thomas/Romain and team)}
\emph{What is the quality of the commits and code produced by hyperactive AI-augmented developers?}

Coding agents accelerate the rate at which code is written but, like human developers, they may introduce bugs, vulnerabilities, and regressions, and they do so at a volume that strains the established mechanisms of human oversight.
When a developer authors tens of thousands of commits over a few months, it becomes implausible that every change receives the kind of careful review that software engineering practice assumes.
This raises a central question:~does the dramatic increase in output we observe in RQ1 come at the expense of the quality of the resulting commits and code, or do agents produce changes that are at least as clean and focused as those of their human counterparts?
He~et~al.~\cite{he2025speed} report that AI-assisted contributions can buy short-term velocity at the cost of long-term maintainability and accumulating complexity, and Xu~et~al.~\cite{Xu2026} document measurable LLM-induced shifts in coding style, complexity, and maintainability across thousands of repositories.
We therefore study commit quality through two complementary lenses, at the \emph{commit} and \emph{code} levels.

\paragraph{Commit-level cleanliness.}
At the commit level, we treat a commit as a unit of change and ask whether it is well-formed: focused on a single concern, appropriately sized, and accompanied by the artifacts (\eg tests) that the nature of the change calls for.
We characterize each commit along three observable dimensions: its \emph{breadth} (the number of files touched and the number of distinct top-level directories, or \emph{modules}, they span) and its \emph{size} (the number of lines added and removed, \ie the patch size).
From these dimensions we derive a set of interpretable cleanliness signals, distinguishing ``clean'' commits (focused changes confined to few files and directories, single-concern patches, and fixes that also modify tests) from ``dirty'' ones (changes scattered across many unrelated directories, very large patches, \etc).

\paragraph{Code-level quality.}
Commit-level signals describe how change is delivered but not the intrinsic quality of the code that results.
At the code level we therefore compute simple, language-agnostic structural metrics: total lines of code, function and method size, cyclomatic complexity, comment density, and the presence and quantity of tests; where tractable, we additionally consider cohesion and coupling proxies.
We compute these metrics on the post-commit state of the changed files so that they reflect the artifacts the developer and agent actually leave behind, and we track their evolution over the observation window to detect whether complexity accumulates as volume grows.

\paragraph{ASAT-based quality.}
Finally, and more exploratorily, we consider a coarser-grained analysis using automated static-analysis tools (ASATs).
Prior work studies agent-influenced quality through such tools~\cite{he2025speed}, but running them per commit is intractable at our dataset's volume and granularity.
We instead snapshot a subset of representative projects at a fixed cadence (\eg once per day) and run an ASAT (\eg SonarQube) on each snapshot, discussing the resulting quality measures and rule violations (code smells, reliability and security issues, adherence to best practices) as a complementary, project-level view rather than a primary metric.
We treat this lens as optional and report it only where it adds insight beyond the commit- and code-level analyses.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
We hypothesize that (H4.1) higher commit volume is associated with more scattered, multi-file, multi-module commits; (H4.2) agent-attributed commits score higher on commit-level structural-cleanliness signals (\eg conventional-commit adherence, focus, test coupling) than unattributed ones, reflecting agents' tendency to produce well-formed changes; and (H4.3) despite this, sustained high-volume agent-assisted development is associated with a gradual accumulation of code-level complexity over the observation window, consistent with a speed-versus-maintainability trade-off~\cite{he2025speed}.

\subsection{RQ2: Coding Agent Usage}

% intro & disclaimer
We report several metrics to illustrate agent usage per-developer and per-task. We note that it is inherently difficult to determine the accuracy of these results. This is because there are several agent harnesses that do not leave signals in commits by default, which means we are not able to clearly distinguish between manually-written and agent-written code. Therefore, some agent harnesses are likely underrepresented -- or not represented at all -- while others are overrepresented.
Additionally, it's important to highlight that the presence of agent harness signals in commits cannot be used to conclude with agent harness was used to produce the code. Depending on the specific prompts and configuration used it doesn't even indicate which agent harness was used to commit the code. But differences in the agent harness signals do at a minimum point to differences in the workflows that lead to these commits, and therefore we think it's worth to discuss these signals.

% - harness frequency (distribution) and diversity
\paragraph{Harness frequency:} Harness frequency showed a clearer picture. None of the developers used more than one detectable agent harness in any significant capacity. Most (Dicklesworthstone, obra, clubanderson, mavam, ruvnet, steveyegge) used Claude Code as their main \textit{detectable} harness, the remaining devs (steipete, philipp-spiess) preferred OpenAI Codex instead. % - harness-switch events
\paragraph{Harness switch events:} Overall we found that agent harness switch events are difficult to gauge. The main source for variations in agent signals between different weeks are changes in the signal to no agent signal ratio, which could either mean that a different harness was used (i.e. one that doesn't leave traces), or commit-level signals were excluded due to nature of the workflow. Notably, many projects exhibit a mixture of new features being added and issues being fixed, with the former possibly being a more manual process and the latter being mostly automated. This could cause discrepancies in commit-level signals, due to different workflows leading to these commits. \subsubsection{Task-Agent Coupling}

% task-agent coupling
In addition to which agent harnesses are used, we also report which tasks are done by which agents, and which agents are preferred for certain tasks. This is based on detecting keywords corresponding to those defined in the Conventional Commits specification, as explained in earlier. We note that the overwhelming majority of these commits either contain signals for Claude Code (46\%) or no signal at all (51.5\%), which makes a global analysis of agent-task-distribution highly biased. For completeness, the analysis shows that the tasks \texttt{build}, \texttt{ci}, \texttt{docs}, \texttt{perf}, \texttt{refactor} and \texttt{revert} usually show no harness attribution, while tasks \texttt{chore} and \texttt{feat} are primarily attributed to Claude Code. The remaining tasks are tied between Claude Code and no harness signal, with no other harness coming close. \autoref{fig:agents-by-commit-type-percentage} shows shows this distribution in a single plot. Therefore, we report the task distribution for each harnesses independently.

\autoref{fig:commit-types-by-agent-percentage-log} shows the general distribution of tasks per agent harness, along with the amount of commits for each harness in our dataset. It's apparent that there are significant differences between agent harnesses with regard to them producing contentional commits or not. For example, GitHub Copilot has an \texttt{unknown} share of 90\%, while Claude Code only has a share of 10\%, and others even lower than that. Additionally, some agent harnesses are dramatically underrepresented in terms of commits (e.g., there's only a single commit associated with the Windsurf agent), which limits the empirical conclusions we can draw from this data.
% TODO compare against no signal commits and all commits, to see if there are substantial deviations
The top 3 tasks among all agent harnesses, as well as all commits without an agent harness signal, and all commits in general, are always found within the \texttt{feat}, \texttt{fix}, \texttt{docs}, \texttt{chore}, and \texttt{refactor} tasks. This could point to a general popularity of these specific tasks when writing commit messages, or generally common tasks. Some tasks, like \texttt{revert}, \texttt{style}, \texttt{perf}, and \texttt{ci}, are rarely used at all, and therefore have many harnesses with no commits referencing those tasks.
Especially the \texttt{feat} and \texttt{fix} tasks are well represented across all agent harnesses. Many harnesses only show minor differences in task distribution, but there are exceptions. Qwen Code has a limited set of only four tasks that appear in commit messages: \texttt{feat}, \texttt{fix}, \texttt{docs}, and \texttt{chore}, with \texttt{docs} having the largest share by far. Cursor is the same, but with \texttt{test} instead of \texttt{docs}. For Codex the \texttt{docs} task also had the largest share, but most other tasks were represented too.
Copilot has near-zero references of most tasks, with only \texttt{feat}, \texttt{fix}, and \texttt{chore} making significant appearances. It also had the lowest share of conventional commits of any harness, whereas Qwen Code exclusively produced conventional commits.

\subsection{RQ3: Repository Switching Behavior}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq4-four-devs} illustrates the repository-switching behavior of four representative developers: Dicklesworthstone and steipete as examples of high-switching developers, and teamchong and steveyegge as examples of more average-switching developers.

Across all developer-weeks, developers maintained activity in a mean of 7.20 repositories. However, repository breadth varied substantially between developers. Dicklesworthstone and steipete maintained activity across a large number of repositories (means of 28.15 and 11.28 active repositories per week, respectively), whereas most other, such as teamchong and steveyegge, typically worked on a smaller set of repositories (means of 2.45 and 1.78).
%   - Per-developer (min, max, mean, median active repos/week):
%     - Dicklesworthstone — 0, 93, 28.15, 26.5
%     - steipete          — 1, 55, 11.28, 7.5
%     - teamchong         — 0, 8, 2.45, 2.0
%     - mavam             — 1, 15, 5.70, 5.0
%     - steveyegge        — 0, 3, 1.78, 2.0
%     - obra              — 0, 11, 4.24, 4.0

Repository activity was moderately concentrated overall, with a median weekly Gini coefficient of 0.449. Gini values near 1 indicate that activity is concentrated in a small number of repositories, whereas values closer to 0 indicate a more even distribution. Developers with the largest number of active repositories also exhibited the highest concentration (e.g., Dicklesworthstone: 0.686; steipete: 0.748), suggesting that although they maintained many repositories concurrently, most activity remained focused on a few dominant projects. In contrast, developers with fewer active repositories generally showed lower concentration (e.g., teamchong: 0.350; steveyegge: 0.092).
% - Selected per-developer median Gini: Dicklesworthstone 0.686; steipete 0.748; teamchong 0.350; mavam 0.483; steveyegge 0.092; obra 0.488.

Most developers only work on a single repository during most sessions, but switch repositories between sessions, with most of them switching multiple times a day (median of 4.0). 
%
While all developers exhibited occasional bursts of switching activity, a small number of developers showed exceptionally high daily switching rates, including Dicklesworthstone (median: 168.5; maximum: 1292 switches/day) and steipete (median: 18.0; maximum: 355 switches/day), suggesting highly parallelized workflows.
% - Day-level maxima: Dicklesworthstone = 1292, steipete = 355, clubanderson = 194, teamchong = 154.
% - Day-level medians: Dicklesworthstone = 168.5, steipete = 18.0, teamchong = 2.0.
% - Session-level medians: Dicklesworthstone = 4.0; most other developers = 0.0.

Compared to the pre-AI baseline reported by Vasilescu et al.~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky}, the developers in our dataset exhibit substantially broader repository engagement. They reported a mean of 3.7 repositories per week (median: 3; maximum: 18), whereas our developers averaged 7.2 active repositories per week with a median of 5.0 and a maximum of 93 repositories. This increase is not solely driven by a small number of extreme developers, as shown by both the mean and median exceeding the baseline. Taken together, these findings provide evidence that AI-agent-assisted workflows enable sustained activity across a broader set of repositories than previously observed.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Construct}: agent detection relies on co-author trailers and message patterns; developers who suppress signals (steipete) are undercounted, interviews aren't guaranteed to reveal actual developer behavior.
  \item \emph{Internal}: selection bias --- curated cohort; not a random sample; findings may not generalise. changes in frequency or behavior could be driven by external factors
  \item \emph{External}: seven developers, one six-month window; early-adoption period; behaviors may differ post-2026.
  \item \emph{Reliability}: GitHub API rate limits forced sampling on high-volume developers; daily totals may undercount.
\end{itemize}
