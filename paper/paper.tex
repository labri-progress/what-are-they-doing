\begin{abstract}
AI coding agents have enabled a new class of developers who commit code at volumes an order of magnitude above historical norms, yet the commit-level behavior of these hyperactive AI-augmented developers remains unstudied.
We present the first longitudinal, commit-level study of such developers on GitHub, based on a curated dataset of nine individuals observed over eight months (September 2025 to April 2026): 146,940 commits across 455 repositories, with per-commit file-level detail.
We address five research questions covering temporal activity patterns, coding agent usage and co-evolution, repository-switching behavior, the characteristics of individual commits, and the quality of the resulting codebases.
Our key findings are: (1) hyperactive developers follow divergent trajectories, ranging from sustained monotonic growth to sharp burst-collapse patterns, revealing that high volume is not synonymous with sustained engagement; (2) agent attribution is detectable in 48.3\% of commits, with \texttt{claude\_code} accounting for the majority (69,127 commits), yet three of our nine developers show near-zero hard agent signals, two of them despite extreme commit volumes and the third at a far lower volume, exposing a critical gap in current detection heuristics; (3) AI agents enable fluid, within-day repository switching at a scale unseen in pre-agent studies; and (4) commit characteristics diverge substantially between agent-attributed and non-attributed commits; and (5) across the 55 projects built by the cohort, we find no strong evidence of code quality degradation, with some measures improving as code accumulates.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{AI-augmented development \and coding agents \and GitHub mining \and commit analysis \and empirical software engineering}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

AI coding agents such as Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and GitHub Copilot entered mainstream software development in 2025.
Unlike earlier completion-style assistants, these agents plan, edit, test, and commit code with limited human intervention, and they compress the cost of producing software by a significant extent.
Large-scale mining studies document this transition: Robbes et al.~\cite{Robbes2026} found agent-assisted commits in 15.85\% to 22.60\% of 129k GitHub projects, and Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2026} also documented measurable LLM-induced shifts in coding style, complexity, and maintainability across more than 20k repositories.

A visible consequence of this transition is the emergence of a new class of developers whose commit volumes exceed historical norms by an order of magnitude.
Pre-agent baselines are well characterized: Kolassa et al.~\cite{kolassa2013empirical} report that the most frequent GitHub committers average 28 commits per week, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024how} measure a median of 10.5 commits per month for paid developers in the Rust project, and Vasilescu et al.~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky} find that 95\% of developers contribute to no more than 1.5 projects per day.
The developers we study operate far outside these envelopes, producing thousands of commits per month spread across dozens of repositories, with a single developer reaching 19,188 commits in one month.
We call them \emph{hyperactive AI-augmented developers}.

Existing work cannot tell us what these developers actually do.
Population-level studies~\cite{Robbes2026,Xu2026} quantify how many projects adopt agents, and pull-request-level studies~\cite{Alam2026,ogenrwot2026ai} characterize agent-authored contributions, but both aggregate away the individual.
The commit-level behavior of a hyperactive AI-augmented developer remains unstudied: their temporal rhythm, the agent harnesses they rely on, the way they spread work across repositories, and the quality of what they commit.
This gap matters beyond descriptive interest.
If commit volumes this large become common, then reviewer load, repository governance, and the heuristics researchers use to detect agent activity all need to be recalibrated on evidence rather than speculation.

We present the first longitudinal, commit-level study of hyperactive AI-augmented developers on GitHub.
We curate a cohort of nine developers identified through public activity signals, heuristic agent detection, and manual verification, and we collect all of their public commits over eight months (September 2025 to April 2026): 146,940 commits across 455 repositories, with per-commit file-level detail.
We address five research questions, and answer each with quantitative evidence.

\textbf{RQ1: How do commit volumes and activity rhythms evolve over the observation window?}
Trajectories diverge sharply.
They range from sustained monotonic growth to burst-and-collapse patterns in which activity peaks and then falls within weeks, showing that high volume is not synonymous with sustained engagement.
Intra-day curves reveal a five-to-eight-hour slowdown phase consistent with sleep, yet commits continue to land during these rest hours, indicating that agents keep working while the developer does not.

\textbf{RQ2: Which coding agent harnesses do hyperactive developers use, and for which tasks?}
Agent attribution is detectable in 48.3\% of commits, dominated by Claude Code (69,127 commits).
Three of the nine developers show near-zero hard agent signals --- two of them (steipete and teamchong) despite extreme commit volumes, the third (philipp-spiess) at a far lower volume of 914 commits --- which exposes a critical blind spot: current detection heuristics systematically undercount agent usage, and population-level adoption estimates built on them are lower bounds.

\textbf{RQ3: How do hyperactive AI-augmented developers switch across repositories?}
AI agents enable fluid, within-day repository switching at a scale unseen in pre-agent studies.
The cohort averages 7.2 actively maintained repositories per week against a pre-agent baseline of 3.7~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky}, the median developer switches repositories four times per day, and the most extreme developer reaches a median of 168.5 switches per day with a maximum of 1,292.

\textbf{RQ4: What is the structure of the commits produced by hyperactive AI-augmented developers?}
Commit characteristics diverge substantially between agent-attributed and non-attributed commits, in breadth (files and directories touched) and size (patch lines).
High volume and commit hygiene coexist in some developers.

\textbf{RQ5: Does the rapid, agent-driven construction of projects degrade the quality of the resulting codebases?}
Across the 55 projects we track with daily SonarQube snapshots, we find no strong evidence of quality degradation, and even evidence of improvement for some metrics as code accumulates.


To sum up, this paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A curated dataset of nine hyperactive AI-augmented developers observed over eight months, with a documented identification protocol, per-commit file-level detail, and recorded false positives, enabling replication and extension.
  \item Five original research questions covering temporal activity, harness usage and co-evolution, repository switching, commit characteristics, and project-level code quality, and we answer them with quantitative analyses over 146,940 commits.
  \item We show that the commit-level traces used by the research community to detect agent activity miss a substantial share of agent-driven work, an actionable finding for anyone mining AI adoption on code-hosting platforms.
  \item An open replication package with data, analysis scripts, and figures are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/tdegueul/what-are-they-doing}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Average developer behavior on Github pre 2024} \label{sec:baseline}

To understand how the traditional activity of developers looked like and to establish a baseline to compare our results against, we collected the activity metrics described in previous literature, including amount of commits, commit size (lines changed), activity schedule, activity duration, and activity across projects. This mostly covers data collected before 2024, with some studies including data from 2024 partially.

\paragraph{Commit frequency of active developers.}
Kolassa et al.~\cite{kolassa2013empirical} find that the median commit interval is 13.78 hours for 50\% of committers, which gives a frequency of at least 1.74 commits per day for these committers. Committers in the 0-10 percentile of commit frequency display an average of 28.07 commits per week and 4.01 per day. They also find an average of 3.206 days between commits, or 0.312 commits per day.
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024how} also looked at commit data and find a median of 4.4 commits per month for volunteers and 10.5 for paid core developers contributing to the Rust project, with a maximum of around 40 commits per month (1.3 commits per day).
Vasilescu et al.~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky} collected a dataset of "prolific" developers that were active for at least 5 years and contributed at least 500 commits across at least 10 different repositories. They found a mean of 23.89 commits per week (3.41 per day), and a median of 15 (2.14 per day).
Stray et al.~\cite{stray2026copilot} analyze 2 years of commit data from a single Norwegian public-sector agile company, covering 26317 non-merge commits by 39 developers over 105 weeks, yielding an average of 6.43 commits per week or 0.92 commits per day.
Milewicz et al.~\cite{milewicz2019characterizing} look into development behavior within scientific software projects, and find that active contributors spend 1.37 years on average working on a project, and create 116 commits during that time, so around 0.23 commits per day.
GitClear presents an extensive report of the commit-frequency distribution between 2020 and 2024~\cite{gitclear2024git}, drawn from public GitHub profiles that recorded between 100 and 20,000 commits in at least one year.
They report that a "median software developer" (who commits at least once per working day) averages "1.73 commits per working day" (around 1.14 per day on average, with 241 working days per year), that a "median Career Developer" reaches "2.8 commits per working day" (around 1.85 per day), and that senior developers reach a median of 1,563 commits per year (4.28 per day), with the 99th percentile committing up to ten times as much as an average developer.
GitClear account for outliners, but caution that their metric is still imperfect since commit counts are easily manipulated.
While GitHub also publishes first-party statistics on developer activity, these only cover push events, not commits directly~\cite{github2023innovationgraph,github2020octoverse}, so we cannot compare against these figures.

\paragraph{Commit size.}
The amount of lines changed per commit was investigated by Riehle et al.~\cite{riehle2012developer}, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024how}, and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024workrhythms}, which found around 20 LoC per commit, 15-16 LoC for peripheral and 23 LoC for core developers per commit, and around 500 lines changed per week on average, respectively, with a maximum of around 3.5k lines changed per week.
Vasilescu et al.~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky} provide week-level statistics for a set of prolific developers and found a median of 635 lines of code added and 247 removed per week.
Sadowski et al. investigated reviewed code changes at Google, where every commit receives a code review, and found the median number of modified lines per change to be 24.

\paragraph{Work schedules.}
Karyotakis et al.~\cite{Karyotakis2026}, Xia et al.~\cite{xia2022exploring}, and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024workrhythms} all find that most developers follow a nine-to-five schedule, but that a substantial minority extend their hours into nights and weekends.
Karyotakis et al.~\cite{Karyotakis2026} find the share of commits within 10:00--17:59 falling from 60.9\% in 2015 to 55.6\% in 2024, night commits (22:00--06:59) rising from 14.3\% to 17.2\%, Saturday commits from 5.5\% to 6.7\%, and the weekday distribution otherwise stable.
Xia et al.~\cite{xia2022exploring} report the same shape for non-commit activity, with contributors starting around 9:00, peaking at 10:00 and 17:00, and dipping between 12:00 and 14:00.
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024workrhythms} cluster 1.5\,m commits from 110 organizations into four rhythms: 64\% nine-to-five, 3\% flex, 25\% overnight, and 7\% week-night and weekend work.
Claes et al.~\cite{claes2018night} supply the closest comparison for prolific developers, with two thirds of the top 10\% of committers in office-hour clusters and one third in an evening/weekend cluster, and 60\% of commits inside a derived eight-hour window whose median runs 10:00--18:00.
Only Meyer et al.~\cite{meyer2017worklife} report durations: an 8.4\,h workday span against just 4.3\,h of active input time.
The population filters in these studies are moderate rather than extreme, at the top 10-20\% of committers or contributors, so developers at the far tail of the distribution aren't covered in detail.
% Time zone effects are handled three different ways: Zhang et al.\ drop the 9.03\% of commits without time zone information, Karyotakis et al.\ discard UTC+0000 commits unless the author previously committed in another offset, and Xia et al.\ infer each contributor's zone from a 10:00--18:00 local day [2]

\paragraph{Multitasking across repositories.}
Multitasking behavior of developers is also investigated by Vasilescu et al.~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky}, who study prolific open-source developers and find that 37\% of developer-weeks involve contributing to multiple projects, that 95\% of developers contribute to no more than 1.5 projects per day on average, and that 98\% contribute to multiple projects in a day at least once. They group all repositories owned by one user or sharing a name into a single "project", which lower-bounds the number of project switches, so \textit{repository} switching is likely even more frequent. Averaged over multi-project weeks only, they report 2.57 projects per week, an upper bound on the unconditional rate since single-project weeks are excluded; averaged over active days only, they report 1.44 projects per day, with a typical week spanning about 4 active days. Kohl et al.~\cite{kohl2020multitasking} reproduce this on a general industry population and find 2.41 projects per week and 1.49 per active day, again over multi-project weeks. Sarker et al.~\cite{sarker2019developer}, tracing prolific developers over all dev-weeks including those with zero project commits, find a lower 1.64 projects per week and 1.11 per active day.

\subsection{Growth and quality of software projects}
\paragraph{Long-term growth rates.}
Hatton, Spinellis and van~Genuchten measured the size evolution of
more than 4{,}000 software systems and found a compound annual growth
rate between 1.20 and 1.22, that is, a doubling of size roughly every
42 months \cite{hatton2017growth}. At the level of the ecosystem
rather than the project, Rousseau et al.\ tracked the Software
Heritage archive and found that the production of original commits
doubles every 30 months and of original source files every 22 months
\cite{rousseau2019growth}.

\paragraph{Industry issue densities.}
SonarSource publishes the State of Code series
\cite{sonar2025stateofcode}, derived from SonarQube Cloud analyses of
7.9 billion lines of code written by 970{,}000 developers in 40{,}000
organizations between July and December 2024, in seven languages
(Java, JavaScript, TypeScript, Python, C\#, C++ and PHP). Across
445~million issues, they report about 2{,}100 bugs, 1{,}200 security
issues (vulnerabilities and hotspots) and 53{,}000 code smells per
million lines of code; code smells are about 16 times more numerous
than all other issue types combined. They also report
all-issue densities per language, from about 20{,}000 per million
lines in Python to 93{,}000 in JavaScript.

Berciu et al.\ analyzed 57 mature open-source Python projects (3{,}656
releases) with SonarQube Community Edition v10.3 and published the
open issues of every release scan \cite{berciu2026longterm,
berciu2026dataset}. This is the closest baseline to our measurements:
the same tool and edition, a stock of open issues rather than a flow
of surfaced ones, per-release scans analogous to our snapshots, and
the same language as seven of our projects. Their projects are
long-lived (median release window of five years) and of comparable
size to ours (median 48{,}000 lines at the last release, against our
91{,}000 non-comment lines).

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:dataset}

This section describes how we built the dataset that underpins all subsequent analyses: how we identified hyperactive AI-augmented developers (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-identification}), how we collected their commit activity (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-collection}), how we detect coding agent usage in commits (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-agents}), and who the nine developers of the final cohort are (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-profiles}).
The complete dataset, collection and analysis scripts, per-developer analysis notes, and documented false positives are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/labri-progress/what-are-they-doing}.

\subsection{Developer Identification and Selection}
\label{sec:dataset-identification}

There is no public registry of hyperactive AI-augmented developers, and commit volume alone is an unreliable indicator of agent usage (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-agents}).
We therefore identify candidates from five complementary public sources: (1) AI-coding conversations on X/Twitter, where practitioners openly discuss their agentic workflows; (2) GitHub search queries targeting repositories and users associated with autonomous coding agents;
(3) a GitHub-wide commit search for the \texttt{noreply@anthropic.com} co-author address that Claude Code appends by default, filtering users by commit and repository counts;
(4) public activity aggregators, namely Gitista\footnote{\url{https://gitista.com/}} and committers.top\footnote{\url{https://committers.top/}}; and (5) the claudecount.com leaderboard of Claude Code usage\footnote{\url{https://www.claudecount.com}}, cross-referenced with the corresponding GitHub profiles.

Selection from this candidate pool is a qualitative curation process carried out by a team of software engineering researchers, not a single automatic rule.
The inclusion workflow has four steps.
First, \emph{initial screening} uses public activity signals and commit-volume queries to retain unusually active developers who may be using AI coding tools.
Second, \emph{heuristic agent detection} inspects each candidate's commits with the \texttt{agent-mining} heuristics~\cite{Robbes2026} (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-agents}), covering co-author trailers, agent-related commit message patterns, and agent-specific files or configuration traces.
Third, \emph{manual repository and commit review}: we read repository contents, inspect commit histories, and examine commit messages, temporal activity patterns, and bursts of work across repositories.
Fourth, \emph{expert judgment}: when explicit agent traces are absent or incomplete, researchers decide on the overall pattern of evidence, including message style, cadence, sustained commit frequency, and repository-level context.

The dataset is thus deliberately \emph{curated rather than mechanically filtered}.
Explicit agent signals strengthen the evidence for inclusion but are not required: some developers leave strong machine-readable traces, while others are included because expert review indicates that their working style is consistent with AI-augmented software engineering.
For transparency, candidates that looked agent-supported at first sight but did not survive manual inspection are documented as false positives in the repository (\eg a Homebrew maintainer whose high commit volume results from routine, partially automated cask updates rather than coding agents).
The final cohort consists of nine developers, listed in Table~\ref{tab:devs} and profiled in Section~\ref{sec:dataset-profiles}.

\subsection{Data Collection}
\label{sec:dataset-collection}

For each of the seven initially selected developers, we first enumerate the repositories they committed to in the last 90 days via the GitHub GraphQL API (\texttt{script/list\_repos\_by\_user\_with\_events.py}); the resulting registry of 260 repositories is stored in \texttt{developers.json}.
For the two developers added to the cohort later (\texttt{steveyegge} and \texttt{clubanderson}), the enumeration is recorded in their monthly snapshot files instead (7 and 100 repositories, respectively; the latter is a lower bound set by the API's repository limit).
Across the whole cohort, we observe commits to 455 distinct repositories.
We then collect every commit authored by each developer in these repositories over an eight-month observation window, from 2025-09-01 to 2026-04-30, using the GitHub GraphQL API (\texttt{script/collect-commits-per-day.py}).
The collector writes one file per developer and month, \texttt{data/\{developer\}-\{YYYY\}-\{MM\}.json}, keyed by day; months whose collection exceeds API limits are split into parts with an additional \texttt{-1}, \texttt{-2}, \ldots{} suffix.

Over the window, the GitHub API reports 151,421 commits for the cohort, of which 146,940 (97.0\%) were retrieved with full metadata and constitute the analyzed dataset.
The 4,481 missing commits are concentrated on the two developers with the highest peak volumes (Dicklesworthstone and ruvnet), where API rate limits forced the collector to fall back to daily totals; the dataset records both counts so that analyses can distinguish reported activity from sampled activity.
Aggregate activity reaches 3,073 commits on the peak day (2026-03-22).

Finally, we fetch per-commit file-level details (the list of changed files with additions and deletions) for every sampled commit via the GitHub REST API and cache them in \texttt{data/commits/\{sha\}.json} (\texttt{script/collect-individual-commits.py}).
This cache enables the file-based agent detection signals and the commit-level analyses of RQ4 without repeated API calls.

\subsection{Agent Detection}
\label{sec:dataset-agents}

We attribute commits to coding agent harnesses using the \texttt{agent-mining} heuristics library~\cite{Robbes2026}.
The library ships machine-readable descriptors for more than 40 coding agents; each descriptor encodes three families of commit-level signals: (1) author names, author email addresses, and co-author trailers (\eg \texttt{Co-authored-by: Claude <noreply@anthropic.com>}), which several harnesses append by default; (2) commit message prefixes and patterns (\eg branch or message prefixes such as \texttt{claude/}); and (3) harness-specific files touched by the commit, such as \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, \texttt{.claude/}, \texttt{.cursor/}, or \texttt{copilot-instructions.md}.
A commit is attributed to a harness when it matches at least one signal in that harness's descriptor; commits may match several harnesses, and commits matching none are classified as unattributed.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\subsection*{RQ1 — Temporal Activity Patterns (@Shane and team)}
\emph{How do commit volumes and activity rhythms evolve over the observation window?}

We quantify development activity as the number of commits produced by each
developer, aggregated by month, day of the week, and hour.
%

To study long-term consistency, we first extract the commit date to determine
the corresponding month. Then, we aggregate the number of commits produced by
each developer per month, sort the results chronologically, and visualize the
monthly activity for each developer using a line plot.

To study weekly work tendencies, we identify the days of the week on
which developers produce commits. To do so, we first extract the name of the day
from each commit date. Next, we aggregate commits by the day of the week. To
account for monthly variance, we stratify these measures by month. Finally, we
visualize the resulting activity distribution for each developer using a heat
map.

To study daily work tendencies, we first extract the hour on which each commit
was made based on their commit date. Then, we aggregate the number of commits
produced during each hour to identify periods of the day when developers are
active. First, we visualize the aggregated activity using a line plot. Next, to
examine periods of (in)activity, we plot the hourly activity for each developer
on the same figure and normalize the y-axis by the proportion of their commits
produced overall. We apply LOWESS smoothing to that line plot to highlight
underlying activity patterns.



\subsection*{RQ2: Coding Agent Harness Usage (@Mira and team)}
\emph{Which coding agent harnesses do hyperactive developers use, and for which tasks?}

% (Fabi): should we consistently refer to it as "agent harnesses" to make it obvious that we're talking about different types of agents () as opposed to just multiple agent instances or sub-agents (i.e. multiple terminals all running an instance of Claude Code)?


% \begin{itemize}
%   \item Motivation; why is this RQ important?
%   \item Metrics: agent signal rate per month, agent diversity per developer, agent-switch events.
%   \item Expected analyses: \texttt{detect-agents.py}, \texttt{plot-agent-commit-coevolution.py}.
%   \item

We analyze repository activity at the commit level and infer the harness used for each commit. We define a \emph{coding agent harness} as the software interface through which a developer interacts with an LLM-based coding agent, such as Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Cursor, or Pi. We define an \emph{agent signal} as observable evidence linking a commit to a specific harness. A commit is assigned to a harness if at least one of the following conditions holds:

\begin{itemize}
\item the commit author name or co-author trailers mention a known harness;
\item the commit modifies harness-specific configuration or metadata files;
\item the commit message contains explicit harness identifiers.
\end{itemize}

% documentation on commit trailers:
% amp has them, yet we basically never see them (95 commits total)
% https://ampcode.com/manual#:~:text=Enable%20adding%20Amp%2DThread%20trailer%20in%20git%20commits%2E%20When%20disabled%2C%20commits%20made%20by%20the%20agent%20will%20not%20include%20the%20Amp%2DThread%3A%20%3Cthread%2Durl%3E%20trailer

A commit without such evidence is classified as \emph{unattributed}. Some harnesses append attribution metadata by default (e.g., Claude Code or Codex co-author trailers), while others leave little or no commit-level trace. Consequently, the absence of a detectable signal does not imply the absence of agent usage. Since this research question requires a harness assignment for all commits, unattributed commits are assigned using a fallback strategy. For each repository and developer, we determine the dominant harness among attributed commits within the surrounding development period and assign unattributed commits to that harness. We evaluate the sensitivity of this approximation with manual validation. We compute the following metrics:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Harness signal rate}: proportion of commits per month containing detectable harness signals.
\item \textbf{Harness frequency}: number and proportion of commits attributed to each harness.
\item \textbf{Harness diversity}: number of distinct harnesses used by a developer within a time window.
\item \textbf{Harness-switch events}: changes in the dominant harness used by a developer or repository over time.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Task classification.}
For task classification, we categorize commits using the Conventional Commits specification\footnote{\url{https://www.conventionalcommits.org/en/v1.0.0/}}. Prior work by Robbes et al.~\cite{Robbes2026} found that agent-assisted commits frequently follow these conventions, making them suitable for commit-level task categorization. For each harness, we compare the distribution of commit categories (e.g., \texttt{feat}, \texttt{fix}, \texttt{refactor}, \texttt{docs}) across development tasks.

\subsection*{RQ3 — Repository Switching Behavior (@Martin and team)}
\emph{How do hyperactive AI-augmented developers switch across repositories?}

Prior work suggests that developers seek to multitask across projects, but that excessive multitasking may negatively affect productivity~\cite{Vasilescu2016sky}. While most developers contribute to multiple repositories at least occasionally, sustained day-to-day multitasking across repositories remains comparatively uncommon.
%
AI coding agents may alter these dynamics by autonomously executing implementation tasks after instruction, allowing developers to work on other repositories while the agent continues operating. Combined with higher-level prompting interfaces that require less continuous repository-specific interaction, this may reduce the practical costs of switching between repositories and enable more fluid multi-repository workflows.
%
A repository switch is defined as two consecutive commits targeting different repositories.
%
We analyze repository-switching behavior using three complementary measures:
(i) the number of actively maintained repositories per developer-week, defined as repositories receiving at least two commits within the week based on prior work~\cite{Damota2022deep}; higher values may indicate broader concurrent activity enabled by lower context-switching costs; %make it a week
(ii) repository concentration measured using the Gini coefficient, where values near 1 indicate activity concentrated in a small number of repositories and lower values indicate more evenly distributed work; and %measured over a week
(iii) repository switch counts at the day and session levels, where unusually high values may reflect highly fragmented or strongly parallelized development workflows. We segment activity into sessions using a 30-minute inactivity threshold, per prior work on developer activity sessionization~\cite{Mozannar2024when}.
%
We report these measures for each developer and compare them with pre-agent
baselines (cf. Section~\ref{sec:baseline}).

\subsection*{RQ4 — Commit Characteristics}

High commit volumes can strain human oversight and may affect the structure of the resulting commits.
Thus, we study the characteristics of the commits produced by the considered hyperactive AI-augmented developers.

\paragraph{Commit-level cleanliness.}
At the commit level, we treat a commit as a unit of change and assess whether it is appropriately sized and focused on a single concern.
We characterize each commit along three observable dimensions: its \emph{breadth} (the number of files touched and the number of distinct top-level directories they span) and its \emph{size} (the number of lines added and removed, \ie the churn).

Churn weighs every changed line equally, whatever the line belongs to. To
further qualify it, we assign each changed file to exactly one of six categories, by analyzingfrom
its path and extension. The categories are: \emph{code} (source files in any of 48 language
extensions), \emph{tests} (files under a test directory, or named as a test),
\emph{documentation} (Markdown, reStructuredText, plain text, and files
under \texttt{docs/} or \texttt{examples/}), \emph{configuration} (YAML, TOML,
JSON, along with build, CI, and lockfile names), \emph{data} (fixtures,
snapshots, corpora, and tabular or binary data formats), and the residual
\emph{others} category.

\subsection*{RQ5 — Project-Level Code Quality}

High commit volumes can strain human oversight and may affect the
maintainability of the resulting code. He~et~al.~\cite{he2025speed} report that
AI-assisted contributions can buy short-term velocity at the cost of long-term
maintainability and accumulating complexity, and Xu~et~al.~\cite{Xu2026} document
measurable LLM-induced shifts in coding style, complexity, and maintainability
across thousands of repositories.
To analyze code quality, we switch from the commit-level perspective of RQ4 to a repository-level perspective.

\paragraph{Code quality measurements.}
We measure software quality with five standard static-analysis metrics extracted from SonarQube.
We normalize the metrics so that projects of different sizes are comparable. The metrics are:
technical debt (SonarQube's estimated remediation effort per kilo-line of code,
kLoC), code smells per kLoC, cyclomatic complexity per kLoC, cognitive
complexity per kLoC, and duplication density (a percentage, used
as-is).

We also extract additional metrics to characterize the growth of projects: number of lines of code, number and size of files, number and size of functions or methods.


\paragraph{Measuring quality over time.}
We consider two scenarios for the evolution of code quality: code \emph{accumulation} and codebase \emph{aging}. For both questions, we estimate the slope of software quality metrics, albeit in slightly different ways. All \todo{which metrics?} five metrics are ``lower is better,'' so a positive trend always means worsening. We estimate the slope with the Theil--Sen estimator \cite{theil1950,sen1968}: the median of the pairwise slopes between all snapshot pairs. We use the Theil--Sen estimator as it makes no distributional assumptions, tolerates up to roughly 29\% arbitrary outliers per project \cite{rousseeuw1987}, and behaves well even with projects with few and irregularly spaced points.

\paragraph{Code accumulation scenario.} \todo{very clear, falsifiable, are the fist one to propose this?} In this scenario, we ask the question ``does quality get worse as more code is written?''. We estimate the slope of each quality measure against the logarithm of the cumulative lines of code ever added. A project contributes a slope for this scenario if it has at least 8 measured snapshots spread over at least 60 days, and its cumulative lines-added counter at least doubles during observation (47 projects qualify).

\paragraph{Codebase aging scenario.}  In this scenario, we ask the question ``does a finished codebase erode as time passes?''. We estimate the slope against calendar days (reported per month,
i.e.\ per 30.44 days), using only snapshots taken \emph{after} a
project has substantially finished building. We define the end of construction per
project as the first snapshot at which the codebase has reached 90\% of its final observed size. A project contributes an aging slope if at least 8 snapshots fall after the end of construction, spread over at least 21 days (32 projects qualify).

\paragraph{Statistics.}
The unit of analysis is the project.
For each metric and each slope type we report the median slope across
projects with a confidence interval, the share of projects with a
worsening (positive) slope, and a sign test on that share. The
confidence interval is the exact, distribution-free order-statistic
interval for the median (sign-test inversion \cite{hollander1973}):
the $k$-th smallest and $k$-th largest of the $n$ project slopes,
with $k$ chosen so that coverage is at least 95\% (96\% at $n=47$,
98\% at $n=32$). A metric is \emph{worsening} if the median's confidence interval lies entirely
above zero, \emph{improving} if entirely below, and otherwise shows
\emph{no clear trend}.

\subsubsection{Harness signal rate}
The weekly agent harness signal shares for all developers are shown in
\autoref{fig:agent-share-weekly}.

\textbf{Dicklesworthstone and ruvnet have high detected harness-signal rates.}
Dicklesworthstone has a mean of 73\% and a median of 80\% per week (STD =
18.5). Ruvnet has a mean of 75\% and a median of 79\% per week (STD = 20.5).
In both cases, nearly all detected signals identify Claude Code.

\textbf{Philipp-spiess, steipete, and teamchong have low detected
harness-signal rates.} Philipp-spiess has a mean of 5\% and a median of 2\%
(STD = 7.75), steipete has a mean of 3\% and a median of 2\% (STD = 2.5), and
teamchong has a mean of 0.5\%, a median of 0\%, and a maximum of 4.5\% per
week (STD = 1). These low rates may reflect disabled attribution metadata and
harnesses that leave no detectable commit-level trace, rather than an absence of
agent use.

\textbf{The other developers have intermediate detected harness-signal rates.}
Mavam, steveyegge, obra, and clubanderson have agent harness rates between 15\%
and 55\% per week. For clubanderson, the weekly analysis begins in December
2025 because the preceding months contain no substantial activity, as confirmed by them after reaching out.
We stress that non-attributed commits (those without agent harness signals) may include work by agents that leave no detectable signals (by default or by configuration), as well as genuine manual work.

% - harness-switch events
\subsubsection{Harness switch events:} The absolute harness-attributed commit volumes including temporal patterns are shown in \autoref{fig:agent-use-over-time}; the percentage view in \autoref{fig:agent-share-weekly} makes changes in the relative harness composition easier to compare.
Some developers had consistent agent harness signals. Developer Dicklesworthstone only had three weeks with less than 50\% of Claude Code signals, and the agent harness share never changed by more than 25\% week over week. The same applies to ruvnet, except for one week with near-zero commit activity. Similarly, philipp-spiess consistently displayed barely any harness signals, with only three weeks where concrete harness signals accounted for more than 10\% of the commit share, one of them exceeding 30\%. In steipete's case, variability was even lower, and just two weeks exceeded 10\% harness signals.
% To quote them: "I literally do not make anything myself. Like everything is agents. [...] and you can't really rely on the commit messages being accurate in terms of authorship."
Most consistent was developer teamchong, with only 8 weeks containing commits with any agent harness signals, and not a single week exceeding 5\% attribution signal rate.
Other developers showed more fluctuation, both in attribution to non-attribution ratio, and in the concrete agent harnesses the signals were attributed to (clubanderson, mavam, obra, steveyegge).
In general, deviations in agent harness signals were often limited to a single week, and appear to be phases were a developer tried out a different harness, and then went back to their old harness afterwards.
Clear, multi-week changes in agent harness signals can only be observed for clubanderson (no harness signals between the weeks of 2026-02-16 and 2026-04-20, with an exception in the week of 2026-03-23) and mavam (a clear change starting in the week of 2025-12-01, and a more gradual change from 2026-02-09 until 2026-03-16).
Overall we found that agent harness switch events are difficult to gauge. The main source for switch events are changes in the signal to no-harness-signal ratio, which might be more impacted by variations in the workflows leading to commits. More work is needed to identify additional signals and heuristics for identifying harness switch events.

\subsection{RQ5: Project-Level Code Quality}
\label{sec:rq5-results}

indeed one may suspect that heavy coding agent use degrades software quality.
We find that for some measures of software quality, this is not necessarily the case.

\paragraph{Code Growth}

\textbf{Project size.}
All 55 projects grow in lines of code, in files, and in functions, at
roughly proportional rates. The median project grows $\times 7.3$ in lines, $\times 6.4$ in files and
$\times 6.1$ in functions; the interquartile ranges overlap
($[\times 2.8, \times 23]$, $[\times 2.5, \times 19]$ and
$[\times 2.6, \times 22]$ respectively). Neither files nor functions
multiply faster than code: the number of entities grows in proportion to
the code they contain.

\textbf{File and function growth}
Over time, the typical function does not grow much. In the median project, the median
function is 9 lines at first observation and 10 lines
at the last ($\times 1.08$). The typical file grows more, from 135 to 245
lines ($\times 1.6$), rising in 84\% of projects. The situation for outliers is different:
The largest file of the median
project grows $\times 4.4$ (from 1{,}029 to 4{,}711 lines) in 95\% of
projects; the largest function grows $\times 3.6$, in
93\%. Beyond the single largest unit, the share of
code in files larger than 1{,}000 lines doubles, from 22\% to 46\% of
non-comment lines, rising in 77\% of the 52 projects that ever have
such a file. Large functions remain rare (functions over 100 lines are
about 2\% of all functions), but the share of function code they
contain rises from 14\% to 19\% (62\% of projects). At the extremes of
the corpus, the largest file reaches 129{,}000 lines and the largest
single function 27{,}700 lines.

\textbf{Summary.}
Overall, the hyperactive developers add many functions of constant size, while file
sizes drift upward and a growing share of code concentrates in a few
oversized files. The bulk of the code keeps the size
distribution it started with; the tail does not.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{External verification.}
In order to further validate our findings, we reached out to all of these developers, receiving a response from seven of them (Dicklesworthstone, steipete, steveyegge, clubanderson, teamchong, ruvnet, and obra). All of them have confirmed that they do make extensive use of coding agents, several even disclosed that they don't write any code manually anymore.
When it comes to the correctness of the specific agent harness attribution, the general concensus was that most harnesses don't disclose themselves by default (aside from Claude Code), and an absence of agent harness indicators should not conclude that code was written manually.

Some of the developers also had workflows involving specific agent harnesses for orchestrating and committing, while other harnesses were used for the actual implementation work. Specifically, Dicklesworthstone mentioned that the implementation is split roughly 50/50 between Claude Code and OpenAI Codex, but Claude Code aggregates and commit changes, while most code is produced with Codex. Claude Code signals therefore describe their commit workflow rather than overall harness use, revealing that the results discovered through commit mining is not always representative of the actual harness usage.

Developer teamchong disclosed that they deliberately turned of the commit attribution in Claude Code, which is the agent harness they use "almost exclusively".
Dicklesworthstone additionally mentioned that they regularly used Google's Antigravity harness with a Gemini model for code review, but never for implementation work, corroborating the task-specific harness selection we observed.
Overall this reveals that the agent harness traces visible on GitHub are not a reliable indicator of actual usage on their own.

\subsection{Implications for harness developers}
% - harness attribution sends a strong and easy-to-detect adoption signal, but isn't yet comparable among harnesses
% - agent harnesses visibility is limited by commit traces being generated by the committing harness, hiding harnesses that are used heavily for implementation work but not for creating commits
% - there are strong discrepancies between harnesses with regard to adhering to the conventional commit specification
% - our results show that harnesses are most frequently used for adding new features, fixing bugs, adding or maintaining documentation, refactoring existing implementations, or performing routine maintenance tasks
Harness attribution provides a strong and easily detectable signal of adoption, yet this signal remains fragmented and non-comparable across different tools. Furthermore, the visibility of individual harnesses is skewed toward the "last mile" of the workflow; because commit traces are generated by the committing harness, tools used heavily for implementation but not for final commits remain hidden. This lack of standardization extends to the commit messages themselves, where we observe strong discrepancies in adherence to the conventional commit specification. Given that harnesses are most frequently used for adding features, fixing bugs, maintaining documentation, and routine refactoring, harness developers should prioritize, stabilize and standardize these core use cases.

\subsection{Implications for AI\&SE researchers}
% - depend less on deliberate harness disclosure, find more robust signals
%   - current heuristics potentially fail to capture a significant portion of agent harness driven work
%   - some devs had workflows involving public agent harness interaction logs
% - multi-harness workflows are frequently condensed into uniform commits that hide away harness distribution of the actual implementation
% - when measuring adoption, agent harness usage has to be normalized per developer to avoid introducing bias from individual hyperactive developers
% - difficult to distinguish between unattributed agent harness work and genuine manual work
%   - although statements from devs paint a picture of near-zero manual implementation work overall
Our findings highlight a significant visibility gap in current research approaches: because heuristics often fail to capture a large portion of agent-driven work, researchers should move away from relying on deliberate harness disclosure in favor of more robust signals, such as public interaction logs which some of the developers mentioned they record. The difficulty in distinguishing unattributed agent work from genuine manual effort is notable as well, since developer statements suggest that manual implementation may actually be near-zero. This data is further obscured by multi-harness workflows, which are often condensed into uniform commits that mask the actual distribution of tools used during implementation. This contributes to the need of normalizing harness usage per developer when measuring adoption, to prevent bias introduced by a small number of hyperactive individuals.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Construct}: agent harness detection relies on co-author trailers and message patterns; developers who suppress signals (steipete) are undercounted, statements from dev outreach isn't guaranteed to reveal actual developer behavior.
  \item \emph{Internal}: selection bias --- curated cohort; not a random sample; findings may not generalise. changes in frequency or behavior could be driven by external factors
  \item \emph{External}: nine developers, one eight-month window; early-adoption period; behaviors may differ post-2026.
  \item \emph{Reliability}: GitHub API rate limits forced sampling on high-volume developers; daily totals may undercount.
\end{itemize}

We note that the related work and baseline figures presented do not align perfectly with the metrics employed in this study. For instance, repository switching is categorized as "project" switches in the baseline, which explicitly excludes transitions between different repositories owned by the same user, the primary behavior observed among our participants. While we have attempted to mitigate these discrepancies by providing ranges and specifying lower and upper bounds, we advise caution when directly comparing these results.


\subsection{Limitations for code quality (RQ5)}
\label{sec:limitations}

Since we start from a small list of hyperactive developers, this study is based on a small sample of short-lived projects. The conclusions describe this population only: they should not be extended to open-source projects in general, nor to lighter uses of coding agents. With this limited number of projects, our confidence intervals are relatively broad: larger studies may observe more precise effects. Importantly, one developer (\textbf{Dicklesworthstone}) contributes 41 of the 55 projects, which further limits the representativeness of the results.

Each project's slope is an estimate, with a degree of imprecision. The study does not rely on any single estimate: it draws conclusions only from the collection of slopes, which mitigates this imprecision at the group level.

Throughout, we quantify``quality'' with five static-analysis measures; the conclusions concern the measures, not quality in any broader sense. In particular, these measures of quality are very local: they are computed within individual files and functions, and none of them describes how the codebase is organized as a whole. Higher level measures of quality (such as architectural quality), may markedly differ.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Adoption of coding agents at scale.}
Mining studies measure how far coding agents have spread in open source.
Robbes et al.~\cite{Robbes2026} conduct the first large-scale study of coding agent adoption on GitHub. They detect agent-assisted commits in 15.85\% to 22.60\% of 129k projects, and find that these commits are larger than other commits and more often add features or fix bugs.
Their \texttt{agent-mining} heuristics are the detection instrument of our study (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-agents}).
Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2026} measure LLM-induced shifts in coding style, complexity, and maintainability across more than 20k repositories linked to arXiv papers.
Both studies count projects, while we follow individual developers, which is how we observe that the detection heuristics miss a substantial share of agent-driven work (Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results}).

\textbf{Provenance of LLM-generated code.}
Yu et al.~\cite{Yu2024} collect 696 snippets of self-admitted LLM-generated code from 229 GitHub projects in five languages, and characterize how developers disclose this provenance.
Self-admission is a voluntary signal, like the co-author trailers of agent harnesses.
Our developer outreach (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) shows that most harnesses do not emit such signals by default, and that developers deliberately turn them off.

\textbf{Agent-authored pull requests.}
A second line of work studies the pull requests opened by agents.
Alam et al.~\cite{Alam2026} analyze 8,106 fix-related pull requests from five AI coding agents in the AIDev dataset, and investigate why agent-involved pull requests remain unmerged.
Ogenrwot and Businge~\cite{ogenrwot2026ai} use the same dataset to compare how agentic and human pull requests modify code.
In these studies, the unit of analysis is the pull request and its author is the agent; in ours, the unit is the developer who directs agents across many repositories.

\textbf{Velocity and quality under AI assistance.}
He et al.~\cite{he2025speed} find that adopting Cursor in open-source projects increases short-term velocity together with long-term complexity.
Stray et al.~\cite{stray2026copilot} follow 39 developers of one public-sector company over two years, with and without GitHub Copilot.
Our RQ5 revisits the quality question on projects built almost entirely by agents, and finds no strong evidence of quality degradation.

\textbf{Individual developer trajectories.}
All the above studies aggregate over projects, pull requests, or teams.
To our knowledge, no prior work follows individual developers at commit resolution over months in the agentic era.
Our case study of nine hyperactive developers complements the population-level view, because it shows what the extreme of agent-driven development looks like before it becomes common.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented the first longitudinal, commit-level study of hyperactive AI-augmented developers on GitHub, covering 146,940 commits by nine developers across 455 repositories from September 2025 to April 2026.

Hyperactivity is not a steady state.
Commit trajectories range from sustained growth to burst-and-collapse within weeks. Yet, a truly unique phenomenon of software engineering has happened.

Future work will expand the cohort, and characterize the ever changing practices of agentic coding. This paper's contribution is the capture of the September 2025 - April 2026 tranche.
